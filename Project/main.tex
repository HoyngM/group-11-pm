\section{Motivation and Scientific Background}
% ============================================================

Process mining uses the event logs of information systems to analyse and improve processes~\cite{aalst2016}. Predictive process monitoring (PPM) goes one step further and uses historical event data to predict the future of cases that are still running, for example their outcome, so that process owners can intervene before a case ends~\cite{teinemaa2019}. Benchmarks show that black-box models such as XGBoost and random forests predict outcomes accurately~\cite{teinemaa2019}, but they do not show why a case gets a certain prediction. Without that insight an analyst cannot judge whether a prediction can be trusted or what to change in the process. Explainable AI (XAI) addresses this problem~\cite{arrieta2020}, and post hoc methods do so for models that are already trained. Existing post hoc methods show which activities influence a prediction, but not whether it matters \emph{where} in the trace an activity is executed. Vazifehdoostirani et al.~\cite{vazifehdoostirani2024} close this gap with the location permutation importance. We reproduce their technique and change the way the activities to test are chosen (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).

\paragraph{Use case.}
Consider the hospital example of Vazifehdoostirani et al.~\cite{vazifehdoostirani2024}. A model predicts whether a sepsis patient will return to the emergency room within 28 days of discharge. Almost every patient receives a lactic acid measurement at some point, so an explanation based on the occurrence of activities would rate this activity as unimportant. The data show otherwise. Patients who receive the measurement as their second activity tend to return, while patients who receive it later tend not to. The relevant information is therefore not the activity itself but its position in the trace. An explanation that captures the location of activities makes this visible to the clinician. An explanation that only ranks activities does not.

\paragraph{Embedding in the course.}
The course starts from the event log and uses it in two directions. Looking back, process discovery builds a model of how the process has run and conformance checking compares that model with the log. Looking ahead, predictive process monitoring trains a model on the log to predict what will happen to a running case, for example its outcome. Our work belongs to the second direction and adds the explainability step that the course presents together with it. The technique does not improve the prediction. It takes the trained model and asks what it has learned from the order of the activities in a trace, that is, from the control-flow perspective of the log.



\paragraph{Commonalities and differences.}
Groups 7, 9 and 10 share our starting point: an event log of finished cases, used to say something about cases that are still running. Groups 9 and 10 do predictive process monitoring as we do, but they change how the prediction is made. Group 9 predicts the remaining time of a case with trace clustering and language models, and group 10 adds inter-case features, information about the other cases in the process at the same moment, to improve the predictions. Group 7 goes one step further than prediction. It uses process mining to build a decision process from the log and reinforcement learning to find the actions that give the best reward. We leave the model as it is and do not act on its output. We explain it. Where group 10 asks whether extra information makes the model better, we ask which information the model already relies on, in particular where the activities occur in a trace.

\section{Conceptual Approach}
\label{sec:approach}
% ============================================================

We reproduce the post hoc XAI technique of Vazifehdoostirani et al.~\cite{vazifehdoostirani2024}. It measures how much the \emph{location} of activities in a trace matters for an outcome prediction model, in the spirit of permutation feature importance: if we shuffle the locations of a group of activities (an itemset) and the model gets worse, the location of that itemset matters. We change one step, the choice of the itemsets, and compare the results (Figure~\ref{fig:approach}).

\paragraph{Input.}
An event log with, for every case, the activities in execution order and a binary outcome label. Timestamps only order the events, and resources are not used. The variant of step 2 also needs case attributes for its case-distance measure (for BPIC11: age, diagnosis, diagnosis code, specialism code, treatment code). Traces are encoded with index-based encoding: position $i$ of the trace is a feature that holds the activity at that position. The method does not depend on the model type, and like the original paper we use XGBoost.

Table~\ref{tab:parameters} lists the parameters and the values we use.

\paragraph{Steps and methods.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline.} Train the model on the index-encoded log and measure its F1-score with cross-validation.
    \item \textbf{Itemset selection.} Choose the activity sets whose location will be tested. The original method uses Apriori. Our variant uses the outcome-oriented patterns of IMPresseD~\cite{vazifehdoostirani2023}. Both give a list of activity sets, and the rest of the pipeline stays the same.
    \item \textbf{Location permutation.} For each itemset, shuffle the locations of its activities over the traces. A new location of an activity must be a location where that activity occurs somewhere in the log (feasibility), and the order of the activities within the itemset stays the same (order preservation). Then encode the permuted log again.
    \item \textbf{Importance.} Score the permuted log with the model from step 1. The drop in F1 compared with the baseline is the importance of the location of the itemset. The shuffle is random, so it is repeated 10 times.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Strategy A: Apriori.} Every single activity is analysed, together with the most frequent itemsets of two or three activities. Apriori searches level by level for sets that occur in at least the minimum support of the cases, using the rule that every subset of a frequent set must be frequent too.

\emph{Strategy B: IMPresseD.} A pattern is a chain of activities in which neighbours are related directly (the second follows immediately) or eventually (it follows later, with at least one event in between). Every pattern is scored on three interest functions: \emph{case coverage} (share of cases that contain it, to maximise), \emph{outcome interest} (information gain of the outcome label from the number of times the pattern occurs in a case, to maximise) and \emph{case distance} (mean distance between cases with and without the pattern based on case attributes, to minimise; this lowers the risk that a pattern only looks important because of, for example, the age of the patients). A pattern is dropped if another pattern is at least as good on all three functions and strictly better on one. The patterns that remain form the Pareto front, so no threshold has to be chosen. Starting from all single activities, the patterns of the front are extended by one activity and scored again, up to length 3. The assignment asks for activity sets, so the ordering relations are dropped at the end: A$\to$B, B$\to$A and A$\Rightarrow$B all give $\{A,B\}$, and duplicates are removed. The patterns are discovered on the training part of the log only, because the outcome interest uses the labels and the choice of itemsets should carry no information about the test cases.

\paragraph{Output.}
For every itemset, a distribution of F1 drops: how much the location of that itemset matters for the predictions. We get this for the itemsets of both strategies, which lets us check whether the same activities and locations come out as important, and how many itemsets each strategy needs.

\paragraph{Event log and model.}
The logs are loaded and filtered with pm4py. As in the original code, activity names are normalised and cases with an activity that occurs fewer than twice in the log are removed. The BPIC11 timestamps only record the date (87--89\% of consecutive events in a case share one), so the events are ordered by their event number. The index-based encoding is implemented as one binary feature per pair of position and activity. The model is XGBoost with default parameters, evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation and the weighted F1-score (scikit-learn). During testing, we noticed that some activities in de BPI datasets always have the same location in a trace. Therefore, these activities are not relevant for analysis, since it is not realistic these will change in real-life and therefore have no effect on the outcome. To prevent that these activities are taken into account in the calculations, these are left out beforehand. This is either done by checking whether events always have the same position from the beginning, or the same relative position from the end.

\paragraph{Itemset selection.}
Strategy A applies the Apriori implementation of mlxtend to all cases. Strategy B ports the automatic mode of the IMPresseD tool: every trace is a chain graph, outcome interest is computed as mutual information, the distance between two cases is $(m \cdot \text{Jaccard} + \text{Euclidean}_{[0,1]})/(m+1)$ over $m$ categorical attributes and the min-max scaled numeric ones, and the Pareto front is computed with paretoset. As in the tool, patterns with an eventual relation are not extended further. We deviate from the tool in two places: the rule that adds a predecessor and a successor in one step is switched off, so that every step adds exactly one activity, and the case distance uses only the case attributes, as defined in the paper.

\paragraph{Location permutation and importance.}
An activity is moved to a random location at which it was observed; the activities of an itemset are moved one at a time, each to a location after the previous one, so that their order is kept. Every itemset and repetition starts from the original traces, and the F1 drop is measured on the held-out cases of each fold, with the test F1 of the fold as baseline. A single activity that is moved in fewer than three cases gets importance 0.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All random steps are seeded, including the fold split. We first ported the authors' code literally and verified the port on the same data: it gives the same Apriori itemsets, the same permutations (355 of 355 for itemsets, 218 of 218 for single activities) and the same IMPresseD Pareto patterns. We then corrected three places where the original code deviates from the published method: it measured the F1 drop on the training cases, which the model has largely memorised; it never reset the permuted traces, so the importance of an itemset depended on the itemsets permuted before it; and a bookkeeping error in its shuffle moved a wrong event in 25 of the 355 BPIC11 permutations, breaking the order of the itemset in 9 of them. 28 unit tests cover the components.

\subsection{Data}

\emph{BPIC11 f1--f3.} The main evaluation uses the three labelled hospital logs from the BPI Challenge 2011 that Vazifehdoostirani et al.~\cite{vazifehdoostirani2024} used, with the outcome labels of Teinemaa et al.~\cite{teinemaa2019} (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Each log defines the binary outcome with a different labelling rule on the activities of a case; in f1 the rule concerns the occurrence of activities, in f2 and f3 also their order. \todo{Check the exact rule per log in Teinemaa et al.\ and name the activities involved.} We take the logs as provided in the authors' repository and clean them as described in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\emph{Synthetic log and negative controls.} We built a small log of exactly 100 events (15 cases), the size agreed with the lecturer for a preliminary evaluation. Activity $B$ occurs in every case and only its location determines the label: an early $B$ gives label 0, a late $B$ label 1, with 10\% label noise. In the control \emph{shuffled within case} the events of each case are permuted, which removes the location effect; in \emph{shuffled outcomes} the labels are permuted over the cases, which removes every relation between trace and label.

\emph{Simulated logs.} Logs of 1000 cases with 14 to 22 events, filled with 10 noise activities, with three planted effects. Activity $x$ and the block $p\,q$ are placed early or late in the trace with equal probability; an early location raises the log-odds of a positive outcome by 2 and a late location lowers them by 2. The presence of activity $y$ raises the log-odds by 2 compared with its absence. The block $p\,q$ occurs in 30\% of the cases in scenario \emph{rare pair} and in 60\% in \emph{frequent pair}; each scenario is simulated 10 times with different seeds.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}

The evaluation answers four questions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{E1, importance step.} Does permuting an activity whose location determines the outcome lower the F1-score clearly, while permutations in the controls leave it unchanged?
    \item \textbf{E2, selection step.} Does a strategy select the itemsets that were planted? An itemset that is not selected never receives an importance value, so the recall of the selection bounds what the whole method can find. On these logs only the selection is run. \todo{Group decision: also run the importance step on one simulated log, to check that $\{x\}$ gets a high and $\{y\}$ a near-zero location importance.}
    \item \textbf{E3, Apriori versus IMPresseD.} On BPIC11, does choosing the itemsets with IMPresseD instead of Apriori change which activities and itemsets have an important location, and how many itemsets have to be tested?
    \item \textbf{E4, sensitivity.} Does the Apriori baseline change when the minimum support is lowered from 0.5 to 0.2 and the top 10 itemsets are extended to the top 25?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Gold standard.} In the synthetic and simulated logs we generate the labels ourselves, so we know which itemsets matter: $\{B\}$ in the synthetic log, nothing in the two controls, and $\{x\}$ and $\{p,q\}$ (location) in the simulated logs. $\{y\}$ is a distractor: its presence matters but its location does not, so a selection strategy may select it, but its location importance should be close to 0. All other activities are noise. For BPIC11 no complete ground truth exists, so there E3 compares the two strategies with each other instead of measuring accuracy. The labelling rules do give a partial expectation: the activities in the rule of a log, and in f2 and f3 their order, should come out as important.

\paragraph{Metrics and settings.} All runs use the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:parameters}: minimum support 0.5 and the top 10 are the defaults of the authors' code, pattern length 3 is set by the assignment, and 5 folds and 10 repetitions follow the original paper. Every itemset gets 50 importance values (5 folds $\times$ 10 repetitions); we report the mean and, as in the original paper, box plots. The IMPresseD selection uses 80\% of the cases, but the folds are drawn from all cases, so about 80\% of each test fold was also used by the selection. \todo{Group decision (question 5 for Laura): repeat the selection inside each fold, or state this as a limitation and adjust the claim in Section~\ref{sec:approach}.} For E2 we measure the \emph{location recall} (share of $\{x\}$ and $\{p,q\}$ that is selected) and the \emph{existence recall} (whether $\{y\}$ is selected). For E3 we compare the number and sizes of the itemsets, the Jaccard overlap of the itemsets with more than one activity, the overlap of the ten most important itemsets, and the Spearman rank correlation of the importance of the itemsets that both strategies select. \todo{Top-10 overlap and Spearman are not computed on \texttt{main} yet: port them from branch \texttt{job/location-importance-comparison} or remove them here.}

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{E1: the importance step detects a location effect.} The model reaches a test F1 of 0.65 on the synthetic log, against 0.39 and 0.42 on the two controls, so the location of $B$ can be learned and the controls carry no signal. $B$ is only tested via Apriori, because IMPresseD does not select it (E2). Permuting the location of $B$ lowers the F1-score by \tbd{} on average, while no itemset in the controls changes it by more than \tbd{}. With 15 cases each test fold holds only three cases, and the minimum of three moved cases (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}) sets many values in the controls to 0 by construction, so E1 is a sanity check rather than a precise measurement. \todo{Consider a larger synthetic log for E1.}

\paragraph{E2: neither strategy selects a pair whose location matters.} Table~\ref{tab:gold} compares the selected itemsets with the gold standard. Both strategies select the distractor $\{y\}$ in every log, as expected, since its presence matters. Apriori always selects $\{x\}$, because it keeps every single activity; IMPresseD misses it in 2 of the 10 rare-pair logs. No strategy selects $\{p,q\}$ in any log. This failure follows from the selection criteria. Apriori ranks itemsets by frequency, and its top 10 is filled with itemsets that contain the start or end activity, which occur in every case (support 0.75 or higher); $\{p,q\}$ (support about 0.60) is not selected, even in the frequent-pair scenario where it passes the minimum support. IMPresseD scores a pattern on how much the number of times it occurs in a case says about the outcome, together with its frequency and case distance. Because $p$ and $q$ are placed early or late with equal probability, their occurrence says almost nothing about the outcome, so neither is on the Pareto front of the single activities; patterns are only extended from that front, so $p \to q$ is never generated. The same explains why IMPresseD sometimes misses $x$, and why it does not select $B$, which occurs once in every case of the synthetic log. Both strategies thus select on existence, while the method is meant to find location effects (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
